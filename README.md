# How to Remove Vocals for a Karaoke Take

Singing along to the radio is easy. Recording a proper karaoke take is harder, because the original singer is always there, right where your voice is supposed to go. The fix is to take their vocal out and keep the band. Here's how to do that with NeuralSound, plus a few things that make the result sound better.

## Start with the vocal remover

Open the [vocal remover](https://neuralsound.org/vocal-remover) and upload the song. It splits the track into the voice and everything else, then gives you the backing on its own: drums, bass, guitars and keys, with the lead vocal gone. That backing track is what you sing over.

Use the best copy of the song you can find. A clean, high-quality file separates noticeably better than a low-bitrate stream or a phone recording of a speaker. Songs with a lot of reverb on the voice, or live recordings, are harder for any tool, so expect to listen a bit more carefully with those.

## Learn the part before you record

It's tempting to go straight to recording, but it helps to learn the melody properly first. The [instrumental remover](https://neuralsound.org/instrumental-remover) does the opposite of the vocal remover: it takes the band away and leaves the singer on their own. With nothing else in the way, you can hear the exact notes, the phrasing and where they breathe.

A simple routine works well. Listen to the isolated vocal a few times, sing along with it, then switch to the backing track and sing it alone. Going back and forth between the two is one of the quickest ways to get a song into your voice.

## Check the backing track

Before you hit record, play the backing track on headphones at a normal volume. Pay attention to the quiet gaps between lines, because that's where any leftover vocal shows up first. Backing vocals and harmonies are sometimes kept on purpose, since many karaoke tracks include them, so decide whether you want to sing over those or work around them.

If you'd like more control, [music separation](https://neuralsound.org/tools/music-separation) splits the song into vocals, drums, bass and other parts. That lets you turn the drums down while you practise, or bring the bass up if you're struggling to find the key.

## Recording your take

Keep the backing a little lower than you think in your headphones, so you can hear yourself clearly. Record a few full takes rather than stopping at every small mistake, then pick the best one or combine the best sections.

If you're filming a video of the take, for example a cover with you talking to the camera at the start, the [background music remover](https://neuralsound.org/background-music-remover) is useful for the spoken parts. It's tuned for the speaking voice, so you can clean up an intro that was recorded over music.

## A note on sharing

Practising at home with a backing track is one thing. If you plan to post your karaoke take, check that you have the rights to use the original recording on the platform you're posting to.
